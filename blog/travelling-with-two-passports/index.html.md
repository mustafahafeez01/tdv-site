# Travelling with Two Passports: Which One Do You Show?

> Plan which passport to show on each leg of a dual-national trip, with UK, US and Canadian guidance and a family document checklist.

## Key Takeaways

- **Plan each leg separately.** Write down what each traveller needs for the outward journey and the return.
- **Check the country's own guidance.** US, UK and Canadian rules have different conditions and exceptions.
- Keep both physical passports accessible and ask the airline how to record the documents for your itinerary.

![A parent and child looking towards an airport window, holding two passport booklets](https://traveldocumentvault.com/blog/travelling-with-two-passports/cover.jpg)

When you're travelling with two passports, work out which document each country requires, then confirm how your airline wants those details recorded. A short written plan for the outward and return journeys is more useful than choosing one booklet for the whole holiday.

Dual nationals are citizens of two countries. This guide separates airline information from entry requirements for travellers, including parents, with UK, US and Canadian examples.

## Plan the return journey before choosing a passport

Before opening the airline's passenger form, put the outward and return flights on separate lines with their departure countries, destinations and connections. Leave room for the documents and the authority whose guidance you used, keeping unresolved questions alongside the relevant leg.

For a family, add a separate line for each person, even when everyone shares the same booking, and do it with the passports on the kitchen table. Leave enough time to ask a question. Reading two documents side by side is easier than trying to explain the difference while someone else searches the bags.

**Write the document's issuing country beside its number** so a note labelled only "passport" doesn't leave you guessing between two booklets. Use the same labels on your preparation sheet and document copies. When you contact the airline, those labels will also help you describe which document you mean. Keep its answer with the relevant flight.

## The US, UK and Canada have different dual-national rules

### United States

The [US State Department](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/planning/personal-needs/dual-nationality.html) says US dual nationals must **enter and leave the United States on their US passport**. That requirement includes US-citizen children. A US citizen cannot use their other nationality to obtain ESTA, the US electronic travel authorisation, instead.

### United Kingdom

[GOV.UK's dual-citizenship guidance](https://www.gov.uk/dual-citizenship) generally points British dual citizens to a valid British or Irish passport, or a certificate of entitlement, for travel to the UK. British and Irish dual citizens cannot get a UK electronic travel authorisation (ETA).

An airline may accept an expired UK passport issued in 1989 or later alongside a valid passport from a nationality eligible for an ETA. The personal details must match, and the airline decides whether to accept them. GOV.UK also describes an alternative for some people who became British after settling under the EU Settlement Scheme. That route requires checking your immigration status and updating your online immigration account if your passport or identity card has changed.

### Canada

[Canada's guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/visit-canada/dual-canadian-citizens-visit-canada.html) generally requires dual Canadians to have a valid Canadian passport for flights to or through Canada. Canadian-Americans may use a valid US or Canadian passport, subject to entry requirements. If you travel with only your US passport, you also need identification showing Canadian citizenship. Special authorisation has separate eligibility conditions.

These examples show why two families on the same flight may need different documents. For other nationalities or routes, start with the relevant government's dual-nationality and entry guidance.

## Ask the airline how to record both sides of the trip

[British Airways explains](https://www.britishairways.com/content/information/help/faq/passports-and-visas/what-is-advanced-passenger-information) that Advance Passenger Information can include your name, nationality, passport number, expiry date and issuing country. The details it collects go to the relevant government authorities. Its list describes some or all of the information a passenger may need to supply.

If the form gives you one document field and you're unsure what belongs there, ask the carrier about your specific itinerary. Explain both citizenships and the documents you intend to present, including the return leg so the answer covers more than the first flight.

You could write: "I hold passports from these two countries and am travelling on this route. Which document details do you need for each leg, and how should I provide the second passport?" Keep the reply with your itinerary and use the carrier's correction process if something needs changing.

Avoid filling a field with an approximate number or choosing a nationality simply to make the form proceed. Compare the information you submit with the actual data page, then review the saved details. ## Give each family member a document plan

Prepare the passport choices person by person, checking which citizenships each child actually holds and whether their documents are ready for the route. If citizenship is uncertain, get that question resolved by the relevant authority before completing the plan.

Use the sheet below as a prompt for your own notes. It records decisions you've checked; it doesn't decide which passport gives someone permission to travel.

| Traveller and leg | Documents to have ready | Checks still to finish |
|---|---|---|
| Adult: outward journey | Issuing countries and document labels | Government guidance and airline instructions |
| Adult: return journey | Documents needed for the route home | Saved passenger details for this leg |
| Child: outward journey | Their own documents for this route | Citizenship questions and outward-leg requirements |
| Child: return journey | Their documents for the journey home | Return-leg requirements and passenger details |

Add a row for another child or a different connection rather than squeezing extra information into the margin. Leave unanswered questions visible, and write who checked each row so another adult can pick up the preparation without repeating everything.

Keep the passport decision alongside the rest of the family's paperwork. ## A UK-US example shows why the two legs need separate rows

Consider an adult who holds valid British and US passports and is flying from the UK to the US and back. Their preparation sheet can start with the entries below, then add the carrier's specific instructions. This example assumes confirmed citizenship and valid documents; it doesn't settle anyone else's circumstances.

| Leg | Government checks to record | Question for the carrier |
|---|---|---|
| UK to US | US passport for US entry. Confirm any departure requirements separately. | Which details should I record for this flight? |
| US to UK | US passport for US departure. Generally, British passport for UK arrival; consider the published UK alternatives where relevant. | How do I provide the documents needed for departure and arrival? |

Because the State Department's requirement covers US departure as well as entry, this adult needs the US passport on both legs, even when using the British passport for UK arrival.

Prepare separate outward and return rows for the child. Leave the document fields blank until you've checked them, even if the intended answer looks identical to a parent's. Work through a connection on an additional row and ask about that part of the itinerary too.

## Keep separate records for the two booklets

Label copies with the person's name and issuing country, then record the expiry date against that exact document. Keep itinerary notes separately identifiable so an old trip's plan doesn't become the starting point for a different route.

A simple paper list or private spreadsheet can do this, while Travel Document Vault is another option for saving document records and their expiry dates. Use the originals to check the details and keep preparation notes distinct from official permission to travel.

Before packing, read your plan from the last flight backwards. Look for unanswered questions about getting home, then check the outward journey and each person's documents. Keep the physical booklets accessible for the trip, with copies stored as supporting records. Give the other travelling adult the same document labels so you can find either passport without unpacking every folder.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use different passports for the outward and return journeys?

Your plan needs to satisfy each country's rules and the carrier's instructions for each leg. Check the outward and return journeys separately, and ask the carrier how to record the documents.

### Can a US dual citizen travel to the US with ESTA?

The US State Department says US dual nationals must use a US passport to enter and leave the United States. They cannot obtain ESTA using their other nationality instead.

### What if my British passport has expired?

GOV.UK describes a carrier-dependent route using an expired UK passport issued in 1989 or later and a valid passport from an ETA-eligible nationality, with matching personal details. Confirm the conditions and carrier acceptance before relying on it.

### Do children need a separate passport plan?

Check each child's citizenships, documents and route individually. For US-citizen children, the State Department's US-passport requirement applies even when a parent holds another nationality.

### What if the names in my passports differ?

Ask the airline and relevant authorities what evidence and booking details they require before travelling. Don't assume a name difference is acceptable or try to resolve it by entering inaccurate passenger information.
