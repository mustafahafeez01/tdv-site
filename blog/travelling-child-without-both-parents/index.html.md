# Travelling With a Child Without Both Parents: What to Carry

> Sole custody, grandparent trips, different surnames - each changes your child's paperwork. What the airline desk and the border officer each look for.

Source: https://traveldocumentvault.com/blog/travelling-child-without-both-parents/

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Check-in staff and border officers check different things.** Satisfying one does not bind the other, at arrival or on the way out.
- One parent travelling: carry a **consent letter and the child's birth certificate**.
- Sole custody: carry the **custody or court order** and any consent needed from others with parental responsibility.
- Other parent has died: if you have sole decision-making responsibility, Canadian guidance recommends a **copy of the death certificate**.
- Different surname: the child's **full birth certificate** shows your relationship, plus a marriage or divorce certificate if your own name has changed.

Airline check-in staff and border officers look twice at a child travelling without both parents, and that second look is routine child-protection screening, not suspicion of anyone in particular. Which paperwork that is depends on why the other parent is absent: still at home, no longer sharing custody, or no longer alive. Each can call for supporting documents, and a consent letter may still be recommended alongside a custody order.

## The Airline Desk and the Border Check Different Things

Two different people look at the same paperwork for two different reasons, and knowing that saves confusion at the desk. Check-in staff are not deciding whether your child may enter the destination country. They are checking whether the airline is confident you will be let in, because [under long-standing arrangements between carriers and governments](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32001L0051), an airline can be held responsible for flying home a passenger the destination later refuses. That is why check-in agents sometimes ask more than the border officer eventually does: they are protecting the airline against its own liability.

The border officer you meet after landing makes an entirely separate decision, unconnected to whatever the airline decided earlier. Some countries repeat the check on the way out too, not only coming in, so a smooth arrival says nothing about how the exit check will go a fortnight later.

Satisfying one does not bind the other.

## Flying With One Parent: Start With the Consent Letter

If you are the only parent travelling and the other parent is alive, well, and simply not on this trip, the document most borders expect is a consent letter, and we cover exactly what one must say, when it needs notarising, and the mistakes that get letters rejected in our full guide to the [child travel consent letter](https://traveldocumentvault.com/blog/child-travel-consent-letter/).

Two things sit outside that letter and are worth carrying alongside it. Pack your child's birth certificate, since a consent letter names a child and a parent, and the birth certificate is what ties the two of you together in the first place. Some countries also expect the letter to reflect [everyone who holds parental responsibility](https://www.gov.uk/permission-take-child-abroad), not only the parent named on the passport, which can include a step-parent or a guardian depending on your family's legal situation. Whether that applies to you is worth checking with your destination's immigration authority rather than assuming, since it varies by country and by circumstance.

## Sole Custody: Carry the Court Order Too

Sole custody does not automatically remove the need for consent. Canadian guidance recommends a consent letter whenever possible or safe, even when a parenting order lets one parent make travel decisions alone. If the order leaves the other parent with contact or access rights, a letter from them is still worth carrying.

Travel with the order, or a certified copy of it, since it does no good in a filing cabinet at home. Officers who ask are usually looking for a plain answer to one question: does this adult have the legal standing to make this decision for this child. If your custody arrangement changed recently, we'd carry the latest order.

## When the Other Parent Has Died: Carry the Death Certificate

If the other parent has died and you have sole decision-making responsibility, Canadian guidance recommends carrying a copy of their death certificate when accompanying your child.

Keep the certified copy with your passports rather than in a separate file at home, since it is a document that can take time to replace if it goes missing partway through the trip. A printed copy plus an encrypted digital backup covers both a lost bag and a phone that will not turn on. If your child's surname differs from yours, the birth certificate does the same job here that it does everywhere else in this article, showing your relationship with the child, whatever name appears on the passport.

The practical answer stays short: keep the copy close.

## A Different Surname to Your Child: The Birth Certificate Shows Your Relationship

A surname that does not match your child's can prompt questions, so it is worth carrying evidence of your relationship rather than hoping nobody asks. Marriage, divorce, remarriage, and simply choosing not to share a surname at birth are all ordinary reasons for the mismatch, and an officer who asks about it is usually working through the same short mental checklist rather than suspecting anything in particular.

Your child's full birth certificate, the one that names you as the parent, shows your relationship. If your own name has changed since that certificate was issued, a marriage or divorce certificate bridges the gap between the name on your passport and the name on your child's. Pack both even for a routine trip to a place you have visited before, because the same mismatch that gets waved through on one visit can prompt a longer conversation on the next, depending on which officer happens to be on duty that day.

## Grandparents, Relatives and Guardians: Who Should Sign the Consent Letter

When a grandparent, aunt, uncle, or family friend travels with a child and neither parent is on the trip, Canadian guidance recommends consent from each non-travelling person with decision-making responsibility. The format is the same [consent letter](https://traveldocumentvault.com/blog/child-travel-consent-letter/) we cover in full elsewhere, but the signatures generally depend on who has parental or decision-making responsibility, with contact details for each.

The accompanying adult should also carry the child's birth certificate and be ready to explain the relationship in plain terms if asked, since "family friend" tends to invite more questions than "grandparent" even when both are carrying identical paperwork. We'd have the airline's paperwork ready for check-in too.

## If Questions Come Anyway

Even with every document in order, a border officer or check-in agent may still ask a few questions, and it helps to know what that conversation actually is: a short verification, not an accusation. The questions may be simple: where the family lives, how the trip came about, and how the child is related to the adult beside them. A child who can answer briefly in their own words tends to move things along faster than a nervous adult answering for them.

If a phone number appears on a consent letter or custody order, an officer may use it to check the document. Carrying everything covered here does not promise zero questions, but it means that when questions do come, you have an answer ready for each one, which is generally what settles these conversations at the desk.

## One Bundle Per Situation

Every situation above needs its own small bundle of paper, and it is easy to lose track of which document belongs with which trip once a family's circumstances have changed more than once. Use this as a starting point rather than a substitute for checking your destination's own requirements, which is worth doing every time rather than assuming last year's trip still applies, and if you are ever unsure which bundle fits, we'd carry the extra document rather than leave it behind.

| Situation | Documents to carry |
|---|---|
| One parent travelling, other parent not present | Consent letter, child's birth certificate |
| Sole or primary custody | Certified copy of the custody or parenting order, child's birth certificate, any consent needed from others with parental responsibility |
| Other parent has died | Certified copy of the death certificate, child's birth certificate |
| Different surname to your child | Child's full birth certificate, marriage or divorce certificate if applicable |
| Grandparent, relative or guardian travelling, neither parent present | Consent letter signed by each non-travelling person with parental or decision-making responsibility, child's birth certificate, any relevant court order |

Our broader [travel document checklist](https://traveldocumentvault.com/blog/travel-document-checklist/) covers what to pack beyond this specific situation, and our guide to [organising family travel documents](https://traveldocumentvault.com/blog/how-to-organise-family-travel-documents/) walks through keeping every family member's paperwork sorted between trips, not only the one you are packing for now. Whichever bundle applies to your family, pull it together today: match each document in the table above with your child's birth certificate, and put the full set in one place before your next trip gets booked.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a consent letter if I have sole custody of my child?

Carry the custody or court order, or a certified copy. Canadian guidance recommends a consent letter whenever possible or safe, even when a parenting order lets one parent make travel decisions alone.

### What do I need to travel with my child if the other parent has died?

If the other parent has died and you have sole decision-making responsibility, Canadian guidance recommends carrying a copy of their death certificate when accompanying your child. Keep a printed copy with your passports and an encrypted digital backup.

### Can my child travel abroad with grandparents or another relative?

Yes, subject to the relevant rules. Canadian guidance recommends consent from each non-travelling person with decision-making responsibility. The accompanying adult should also carry contact details for each and a copy of the child's birth certificate. We'd have the airline's paperwork ready too.

### Will a different surname from my child cause problems at the airport?

It can prompt extra questions. A copy of the child's full birth certificate, which names you as the parent, shows your relationship. A marriage or divorce certificate helps if your name has changed since the birth certificate was issued. Pack them even for routine trips.

### Who checks the paperwork, the airline or border control?

Both can, at different moments. Check-in staff confirm you meet the destination's entry rules before boarding, because airlines can be fined for carrying passengers who are then refused entry. Border officers make their own decision on arrival, and sometimes on exit. Satisfying one does not bind the other.
