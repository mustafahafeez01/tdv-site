Child Travel Consent Letter: When You Need One and What It Must Say

Travel Document Vault

May 5, 2026 · 8 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Requirements vary significantly by country. A letter that works for one destination may not be accepted elsewhere.
  • Canada strongly recommends a notary public witness the signature.
  • A letter usually includes the child's full name, date of birth, passport number, exact travel dates, destinations, and contact details for the absent parent.
  • Common rejections come from missing information, lack of notarisation or outdated signatures.
  • Carry a printed copy as the primary document, and keep an encrypted digital backup for emergencies.

A consent letter does one job at the airline desk: it shows that the adult travelling with a child has the absent parent's agreement. Most are accepted without comment.

The ones that cause a delay rarely fail on substance. They fail on a small detail, usually a passport number that changed at the child's last renewal.

That scenario, and thousands like it, happens because child travel consent letters are among the most commonly misunderstood travel documents. Rules vary widely by country, and a letter that works fine on one trip can fail at the border on the next. Knowing exactly when one is required, what it must contain, and how to keep it valid prevents the most common reasons families get stopped at the border.

Who Actually Needs a Child Travel Consent Letter?

It depends on the countries involved, because there's no global standard, which is why so many families get caught off guard.

A letter may be needed in these situations:

  • A child travels with only one parent. Immigration officers want evidence the absent parent has not objected to the trip.
  • A child travels with neither parent. The letter authorises a grandparent, aunt, uncle, or other guardian to travel with the child. Some countries ask for consent from both parents in this case.
  • A child travels with one parent whose surname differs from the child's. This raises flags at some borders, particularly for younger children.
  • A child is adopted or in foster care. Legal guardianship documents and consent may be required alongside the letter.

Critical caveat: rules vary widely. Canada does not legally require a consent letter, but the Government of Canada strongly recommends one and notes that "failure to produce a letter upon request may result in delays or a refusal to enter or exit a country." The South African Embassy says visa-exempt foreign children travelling with an adult generally do not need a parental consent letter. Some countries apply requirements only to children under a certain age. There is no global standard.

Always verify requirements directly with the immigration authority for your specific destination country before travelling. Use the IATA Travel Centre to check entry requirements, or contact your destination country's embassy. If you are unsure, having the letter is better than the alternative: being stopped at check-in or the border.

What the Letter Must Say to Be Accepted

A child travel consent letter isn't a casual note - it's a formal document, often notarised, that usually contains specific information. Most letters include:

  • Child's full name and date of birth, exactly as printed on the passport.
  • Child's passport number.
  • Proposed travel dates (departure and expected return).
  • Destinations, with all countries the child will visit listed by name.
  • Name and relationship of the accompanying adult.
  • Contact information for the absent parent: full name, phone number, and email.
  • Explicit consent statement ("I/We hereby grant permission for…") signed by the absent parent.
  • Signature(s) of the absent parent(s), with date.
  • Notarisation or certification, if required by destination.

Write the letter in formal language, and avoid vague phrases like "my child can travel whenever". Border officials need to see the absent parent explicitly consents to this specific trip, on these specific dates, to these specific places. For example, you might write: "I, [Full Name], hereby grant permission for my child [Child's Full Name], passport number [number], to travel to [destination(s)] departing on [date] and returning on [date], accompanied by [Travelling Adult's Name and Relationship]."

A border officer or airline may want to reach the absent parent, so make sure that number is correct, is answered by the person named, and that they can confirm permission on the call. If you cannot guarantee someone will answer during border hours, list an alternative contact and note it in the letter.

Rules on Notarisation Vary by Destination

Where a witnessed letter is asked for or recommended, that usually means a notary public or solicitor signs and stamps it, verifying the absent parent signed it in their presence.

What a few countries say about notarisation:

  • Canada. A consent letter is not legally required, but the Government of Canada strongly recommends one and recommends a notary public witness the signature. Border and airline staff may request it on entry or exit.
  • South Africa. The South African Embassy says visa-exempt foreign children travelling with an adult generally do not need a parental consent letter. Department of Home Affairs briefing.
  • Australia. Ask your airline about consent letters for children travelling without both parents.
  • United Kingdom. GOV.UK says permission is generally required from everyone with parental responsibility or from a court before taking a child abroad from the UK. A letter is usually enough to show permission; exceptions apply.
  • United States. USAGov says a consent letter may be needed when a child travels alone or with only one parent or legal guardian.

Notary fees vary by country and by notary, and by how quickly you need an appointment, so ask what it will cost and how far ahead you need to book, especially during school holidays. Always confirm your destination's requirements first. The destination country's rules determine whether notarisation is needed at all, not your home country's.

Common Mistakes That Get Letters Rejected

Even well-prepared families trip up in predictable ways. These are the most frequent:

  1. A contact number no one answers. A border officer or airline may want to reach the absent parent, so give a number that will be answered.
  2. Vague travel dates. Phrases like "summer 2026" are too broad. Use exact departure and return dates.
  3. Unsigned by the absent parent. A letter signed only by the travelling parent is worthless. Both parents (or legal guardians) must sign if both have legal custody.
  4. Missing notarisation when required. Where a witnessed letter is asked for, an unwitnessed one may be refused.
  5. Outdated signature. We'd carry a recently signed letter.
  6. Wrong passport number or child name. Even small mismatches raise red flags and cause delays.
  7. Incomplete destination list. If the letter says "Europe" but does not name specific countries, it can be rejected.
  8. No explicit permission statement. The letter must clearly state the absent parent consents to this trip.

Carry the Original and Keep a Digital Backup

Once the letter is signed and notarised, the next challenge is keeping it safe and accessible during travel, so maintain three versions:

  1. Original signed letter, carried in your passport wallet or travel document folder.
  2. Spare printed copy, packed separately in case the original goes missing.
  3. Encrypted digital backup, so you can re-print if a paper copy is lost or damaged.

The Government of Canada says original signed letters are best and officials may not accept photocopies or digital versions, so carry the printed original and keep the digital copy as a backup. If you also organise your family's travel documents centrally, the consent letter sits alongside passports, visas, and vaccination records, ready for any trip.

Run Through This Before You Travel

In the days before departure, run through this checklist:

  • Verify requirements with each destination country's immigration authority.
  • Confirm all names (including middle names), passport numbers, and dates match across documents.
  • Check that all required signatures are present, dated, and match the named parent.
  • If notarised, verify the seal, stamp, signature, and date are clear.
  • Confirm the absent parent's contact number is correct and they will be reachable while you travel.
  • Carry a printed copy in a protective sleeve in your travel documents folder.
  • Store an encrypted scanned copy as a digital backup.
  • If your child's passport is renewed before travel, obtain a fresh letter showing the new passport number.

Go through this list before you leave the house, not at the check-in desk, because a changed passport number is the detail families miss most often.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a child travel consent letter?

It depends on your destination. Requirements vary by country. A consent letter may be needed when a child travels with one parent, with neither parent, or with a guardian.

Does a child travel consent letter need to be notarised?

It depends on the destination. Canada strongly recommends notarisation but does not legally require a consent letter in Canada. The South African Embassy says visa-exempt foreign children travelling with an adult generally do not need a parental consent letter.

What happens if a child travel consent letter is rejected at the border?

Border officers may refuse entry or prevent the child from travelling. Common rejection reasons include missing information, lack of notarisation, outdated signatures, wrong passport number, or contact details that cannot be verified by phone.

How long is a child travel consent letter valid for?

There is no single validity period. Canada's guidance gives none. We'd carry a recently signed letter, ideally within the last few months, and check your destination's rules. If the child's passport is renewed, get a fresh letter, because the old one will show the old passport number.

Can you store a child travel consent letter digitally?

Don't rely on a digital copy alone. Canada, for one, says original signed letters are best and officials may not accept digital versions. Carry the printed original and keep a secure digital backup on your phone.
