# Schengen Passport Rules for Family Trips: What Parents Get Wrong

> Schengen needs 3 months' validity beyond departure, not 6 - and UK kids' passports last 5 years. What families should check the day they book.

## Key Takeaways

- These rules apply to non-EU visitors on short stays, such as UK families on holiday. Schengen generally requires **3 months of validity beyond the date you leave**, plus a passport issued within the last 10 years - it is not the "6-month rule".
- **UK children's passports expire in 5 years**, so a family's passports fall out of sync and the earliest expiry decides the trip.
- Pre-October-2018 **UK passports with bonus months** can fail the 10-year issuance test even while the booklet still looks valid.
- Airlines generally check passports against **IATA's Timatic database** at check-in, and one failed passport can ground the family.
- **Audit every passport the day you book**, not the week you fly. Five minutes at booking time is the whole fix.

Booking a family trip to Europe usually starts the same way: you check your own passport, see years of validity left, and hit confirm. The catch arrives weeks later, when someone finally opens the drawer and finds that a UK child's passport, issued on a shorter clock than a UK adult's, runs out sooner than anyone thought. Nobody sends parents a warning letter about this. The check-in desk delivers the news instead.

Two rules and one quirk of UK children's passports explain nearly every family Schengen problem, and all three can be checked in five minutes on the day you book.

## Schengen's Rule: 3 Months Beyond Departure

The EU's general requirement is: [your passport must be valid for at least 3 months after the date you intend to leave the Schengen area](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/france/entry-requirements), and it must have been issued within the previous 10 years.

Imagine your family returns from Italy on 20 August. Each passport generally needs validity to roughly 20 November, because that's three months beyond your departure date, not your arrival, and not your booking date. A passport expiring on 10 November fails that test, even though the holiday itself ends weeks earlier.

The issued-within-10-years condition catches UK families in a specific way. Older UK passports could carry extra unused months added from a previous passport, so some booklets show more than 10 years between issue and expiry. Those extra months generally do not count: the passport must be under 10 years old on the day you enter, whatever the expiry date printed inside.

## Why This Is Not the 6-Month Rule

Most travellers absorb the idea that passports need six months of validity to go anywhere. That rule is real: [Thailand asks for six months of validity](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/thailand/entry-requirements), and many other countries apply a version of it. But Schengen doesn't. Schengen generally asks for three months beyond departure, which is gentler, and yet the two get blended into a vague "six months from somewhere" that produces both false alarm and false confidence.

## UK Kids' Passports Run on a Faster Clock

[UK adult passports last 10 years](https://www.gov.uk/apply-first-adult-passport), while [the UK issues children's passports for only five](https://www.gov.uk/get-a-child-passport). That asymmetry is where many otherwise careful plans come unstuck.

A UK family of four therefore operates on four different expiry schedules. The earliest date decides whether the trip happens at all. Two adults renewed together will drift apart from their children's documents within a couple of years, which is exactly why checking your own passport tells you almost nothing about the family's readiness.

For American families there's an extra wrinkle: a child's renewal can need an in-person application and take several weeks, so check your passport authority's current rules and processing times before you book. That is manageable in March but becomes a real problem two weeks before departure. UK families have it slightly easier: find out the current processing times before booking and allow extra time if information is needed.

## Check-in Reality: Timatic Doesn't Round Up

Whatever the border might tolerate, you'll meet the rules earlier than that, at check-in. Airlines generally check passports against IATA's Timatic database, which lists each destination's entry requirements. If your return date needs validity to 20 November and a passport expires on 15 November, the system says no. Expect to be turned away at check-in.

One failed passport rarely strands one person; it grounds the family. Few parents will send three members onward and leave one at the desk, so in practice the whole booking gets rebooked or lost.

## Audit Every Passport the Day You Book

The fix is simple and takes just minutes. On the day you book, gather every passport in the household and note each expiry date and issue date. Ask two questions for each passport: will it still be valid three months after your return, and will it be less than ten years old when you enter? Anything that fails either test goes into the renewal queue that week. At that point, you can still renew, reroute the trip, or shift your dates without penalty, and we'd always choose renewing over waiting to see if the dates move.

Children generally need the same passport validity buffer as adults. One aside for British and Irish trips: Ireland isn't in Schengen at all. UK-Ireland travel runs under the [separate Common Travel Area](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/ireland/entry-requirements), so don't generalise from a Dublin trip to a Paris one.

None of this needs an app. A spreadsheet helps plenty of families, and a twice-yearly calendar reminder handles most of the work. The only non-negotiable: do the audit at booking time, when a short passport is still an errand, not an emergency.

Keep your own passport and visa dates on your phone, with reminders before they expire.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between the 6-month rule and Schengen's 3-month rule?

They are separate rules for different destinations. Countries such as Thailand and [Indonesia](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/indonesia/entry-requirements) generally want 6 months of validity remaining. For non-EU visitors on short stays, Schengen generally requires 3 months of validity beyond the date you leave, plus a passport issued within the last 10 years. Families who've travelled to Southeast Asia often assume the 6-month figure applies everywhere - it doesn't.

### Do children follow different Schengen passport rules?

Children generally need the same 3 months of validity beyond departure and the same issued-within-10-years condition as adults. What differs is the passport itself: the UK issues children's passports for 5 years rather than the 10 years for UK adults.

### When should we renew a child's passport before a trip?

As soon as you book, see whether your child's passport will still have 3 months of validity beyond your return date. If it won't, start straight away. Check your passport authority's current processing times, including UK times for a UK application, and allow extra time if the application needs more information.

### Can we fly while a child's passport renewal is in progress?

No. You need the physical passport in hand to board an international flight. An application won't help at check-in - build renewal time into your booking decision, don't hope it arrives in the final week.

### What happens at check-in if one passport falls short?

Airlines generally check passports against IATA's Timatic database, which lists each destination's entry requirements. If one family member fails the check, that person cannot board - which in practice families usually handle by rebooking rather than splitting up.
